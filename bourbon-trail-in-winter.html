The Kentucky Bourbon Trail in Winter

December through February is the quietest, cheapest stretch of the year on the trail. Here's how to handle the holiday closures, the short days and the back roads, and how to build a route that works with the weather.

8 min read
Updated November 2026

The Short Answer

Yes, go in winter, but plan it differently. The major distilleries run tours all winter, groups are smaller, and lodging is at its cheapest of the year. What changes is the shape of the day: the sun sets around 5:20 PM in late December, warehouses are as cold as the air outside, and a single ice storm can close the county roads that lead to the best countryside distilleries.

So the winter rules are simple. Avoid the four holiday closure days, keep your driving compact, put the longest drive in the middle of the day, and finish each day in your base town before dark. Do that and winter is the most relaxed version of the trail you can get.

If you're still deciding when to go, start with our month-by-month guide to the best time to visit. This page is for people who have already picked winter.

Winter at a Glance (December to February)

🌡️ Highs in the 30s and 40s 👥 Lightest crowds of the year 💰 Lowest lodging prices

Good: small tour groups, guides with time for questions, easy reservations almost everywhere, quiet gift shops, cheap stays.

Harder: holiday closures, short days, cold rickhouses, bare-tree scenery, and the occasional ice storm on rural two-lane roads.

Holiday Closures: The Days to Avoid

The closures that are close to universal on the trail are Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and New Year's Day. Jim Beam's tour page, for example, lists exactly those four (plus Easter Sunday). Build your trip around them and you avoid the most common winter mistake, which is driving to a locked gate.

Beyond those four, it varies by distillery:

  • Thanksgiving weekend: most distilleries reopen the Friday after Thanksgiving, but not all of them. It can also be busier than you'd expect, because families home for the holiday go out on tours together.
  • Christmas to New Year's: the riskiest week of the winter. Some distilleries close New Year's Eve as well, and some take a longer break between the holidays. Check every stop on your list individually for any date from December 24 to January 1.
  • January and February: the big names hold their normal schedules, but smaller craft distilleries are the likeliest to cut days or close early. Confirm hours on the distillery's own site the week you travel, not the month before.
Check the Source

Holiday hours change every year and third-party listings, including Google, are often a season behind. The distillery's own booking page is the only reliable answer. If a date isn't bookable there, assume the distillery is closed that day.

Short Days Change the Route

This is the winter factor most people don't plan for. Most distilleries run their last tours in mid-afternoon anyway, and in December and January the light goes not long after. That matters because the countryside distilleries sit on unlit two-lane roads, the kind that are a pleasant drive at noon and a tense one in the dark after a tasting. Your designated driver will thank you for planning around it.

The fix is a compact route:

  • Base yourself in the middle. Bardstown and the countryside toward New Hope put the core of the trail within a short drive, so no single day needs a long return leg.
  • Book the early slot for your farthest stop. Drive out in the morning and work back toward your base through the afternoon.
  • Keep it to two or three stops a day. That is the honest ceiling in any season, and in winter it leaves room for slower roads.
  • Save walkable stops for the evening. Louisville's Whiskey Row is the one part of the trail that works after dark, which makes it the natural first or last night of a winter trip.

Winter Driving: Ice Is the Real Risk

Kentucky doesn't get much heavy snow, but it does get ice, and ice is what shuts things down. The interstates and parkways are treated first. The rural roads out to Maker's Mark in Loretto, Four Roses and Wild Turkey in Lawrenceburg, or Woodford Reserve in Versailles can stay slick for longer.

  • Watch the forecast the night before, not just the day of. Freezing rain overnight is the scenario to worry about, because it leaves glazed roads by morning even after a mild day.
  • Distilleries do close for weather. If there's an ice warning, check the distillery's site and social accounts before setting out.
  • Leave one flexible half-day in a multi-day trip. If the weather turns, you can swap a countryside stop for a Louisville or Bardstown one without losing a whole reservation.
  • If you're hiring a driver, ask the company what their weather policy is when you book. Our transportation guide covers the options.

Cold Rickhouses: Dress for Outdoors

Bourbon ages in warehouses that aren't heated or cooled; that temperature swing is part of how the whiskey ages. In summer it makes the upper floors brutal. In winter the opposite happens: the inside of a rickhouse is about as cold as the outside.

That's not a reason to skip the warehouse tours. Without the heat they're actually more comfortable than in July, the bourbon smell is just as strong, and you can stand still and listen without sweating. Just treat the tour as time outdoors. Wear a real coat, not a fleece. Bring gloves and wear proper shoes, because you'll be on gravel paths and concrete floors and walking between buildings. Layers win, because the tasting rooms and visitor centers are warm.

Booking Is Easier, With One Exception

Winter is the easiest season to get tours. At most distilleries a week or two ahead is plenty, walk-ups work more often than at any other time of year, and the slots you do book tend to have smaller groups. Midweek in January is about as uncrowded as the trail ever gets.

The exception is Buffalo Trace. Its tours are free, which is why they're the hardest reservation in Kentucky in every season. New dates are released every Wednesday at 10:00 AM Eastern, each covering a week about eight weeks out. Count back eight weeks from your trip and put that Wednesday in your calendar. For everything else, our booking guide lists each distillery's lead time.

The Holiday Season on the Trail

December has its own appeal. Several distilleries run holiday events, seasonal tastings or extended gift shop hours, and downtown Bardstown does the holidays well. These events change every year, so check each distillery's events page for this year's schedule rather than relying on last year's.

It's also a good season for gift shop shopping. A bottle you can only buy at the distillery makes a better present than anything on a store shelf back home, and winter lines are short. If Buffalo Trace is on your list, our Buffalo Trace gift shop guide explains how its rotating bottles work.

A Winter Route That Works

Our 3-day itinerary works in winter with a few adjustments to the order and the timing:

  • Louisville day: Whiskey Row is built for winter. Evan Williams, Old Forester and Michter's are a short walk apart, so you spend very little time out in the cold. A few of the Whiskey Row tasting rooms stay open until 7 PM, which fills the dark early evening. The Whiskey Row walking guide has the route.
  • Bardstown day: the most winter-proof day on the trail. Heaven Hill, Willett, Lux Row and Bardstown Bourbon Co. are all a short drive from town, so even an icy morning rarely sinks the whole day. If Maker's Mark is on your list, take the earliest tour you can.
  • Frankfort day: book Buffalo Trace's earliest available slot, then head back toward your base before dark rather than adding a late stop.

Where to Stay in Winter

Staying central matters more in winter than in any other season, because it keeps every return drive short. Bardstown or the countryside toward New Hope puts most of the core distilleries within about 20 minutes. Our where-to-stay guide compares the options.
